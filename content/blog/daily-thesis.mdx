Daily Thesis started from a fairly specific annoyance: keeping up with new
papers in CS, medicine, and biology meant either subscribing to a dozen
noisy feeds or checking arXiv manually and forgetting to. I wanted something
closer to a feed — pick your topics once, then get a stream of papers worth
reading, without the app becoming another thing to manage.

## The shape of the app

The core loop is simple on purpose:

- **Onboarding** — pick topics once, up front.
- **Feed** — swipe through papers, save the ones worth returning to.
- **Reading** — open a paper with quick actions and dark mode, so reading
  doesn't mean leaving the app for a PDF viewer.
- **History** — everything you've read or saved, kept locally.

Everything is built in Flutter, which made the cross-platform decision easy —
the same codebase covers Android, iOS, and desktop without much extra work
per platform.

## No backend, on purpose

The bigger architectural decision was keeping all per-user data — reading
history, saved papers, streaks — on-device only, in SQLite/Hive, with no
server behind it. That has real trade-offs: no syncing across devices, no
server-side analytics, nothing to fall back on if local storage gets wiped.

But for a tool that's fundamentally "help me read more papers," it also
means no accounts, no login friction, and nothing about what someone reads
ever leaves their phone. For a research app, that felt like the right
default rather than something to bolt on later as a privacy feature.

## Making unfamiliar papers less intimidating

The feature I've spent the most time on recently is what I've been calling
the **AI Context Window** — a collapsible "New to this field? Tap for
background" panel on each paper. Open it and you get a plain-language
summary of the paper plus a glossary of the jargon inside it — things like
sensitivity/specificity, circulating cell-free DNA, or z-scores, depending
on the field.

The idea is that the feed shouldn't only work for people already fluent in
a field's vocabulary. A CS paper's methods section and a biology paper's
methods section assume completely different background knowledge, and the
app should meet a reader wherever they're starting from instead of assuming
expertise.

## What's half-built right now

The site that recruits beta testers is currently out of date — it still
shows the original feature set (topic onboarding, reading, history, an
"extra features" carousel) but not the Context Window work or the streak
system I'm building now: streaks, freeze/grace days, and milestones, meant
to nudge toward the habit of actually opening the app regularly rather than
letting papers pile up unread.

## What's next

Getting the marketing site to reflect what the app actually does is
overdue. After that, the real test is testers — recruiting more of them
through the Google Group and seeing whether the Context Window and the
streak mechanics actually change reading behavior, or just look good on
paper.
